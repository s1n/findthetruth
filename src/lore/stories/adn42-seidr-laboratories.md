## Seidr Laboratories

“What we’re talking about here is the end of the bioroid,” said Feder. “A bioroid cannot do what you’re describing. By definition. If it could, it wouldn’t be a bioroid.” Eyes rolled. Moon’s in particular. This was not the first time Feder had held forth on the topic. It was not, in fact, the first time during this meeting.

“Look, put aside the technical concerns,” said Nascez.

“We cannot ‘put aside’ the technical concerns!” protested Feder again. “The Directives are fundamental to—”

“To the public reception of bioroids; yes, I quite agree,” said Nascez. Feder collapsed back in his chair, fuming. “People are terrified of the bogeyman of the ‘killer robot,’ of technology they cannot control. The Directives are the only reason the public accepts bioroids at all.”

Feder saw his chance and jumped back in. “Without Directives, a bioroid would be no more ‘programmable’ than a human being. The public knows this.”

“But if we are successful, then the public will be wrong,” said Moon. “We are attempting something new. That is, in fact, our entire purpose. I thought we all understood that.”

“We all understand that,” said Amrit. He spread his hands, as if to press back the hostility at the luminous glass table. “But what we are less united on is whether this directive-less neural channeling initiative is possible.”

“Of course it’s possible.” Strauss threw her hands up in the air. “Everyone in the room is using a directive-less brain to think with right now.”

“Copying a human brain has proved unfeasible thus far,” said Feder. At least on technical issues he could be calm and almost pleasant. “Nor, were it possible, would it be useful. An unregulated, uncontrolled intelligence isn’t a tool that we can sell on the market. It’s...” He floundered, waved a hand dismissively. “Useless.”

“It won’t be unregulated or uncontrolled,” said Moon. “Bioroid brains can learn. We can teach our new model ethics: teach it to, in short, control itself. The new bioroid won’t need directives. It will have instincts.”

An angry red icon appeared in the glass of the meeting-room table. Moon cut off the next round of protests with a wave of her hand. “Thank you for your input, everyone; we are out of time. I’d like to remind you that this project was given to us by Ms. McGuire herself, and if you don’t want to work on it, you can look for a new job. As always, this meeting has been a complete waste of my time. Good-bye.” She wondered if her secretary had edited out that last bit as the other attendees derezzed and vanished. She supposed she’d find out when HR sent her another round of angry demands.

“The Director calling for you, Ms. Moon,” said her secretary as calmly as if the Director called on a regular basis. Moon kept her scream internal. The Director calling could mean many things, and hardly any of them ended with Moon fired. She stood, smoothed her skirt and gave her hair a quick check.

“I’ll take it here,” Moon said.

The Director was suddenly standing over the foot of the table, leaning forward in her trademark power pose. “Estelle,” she said. “Good. Something’s gone wrong with Project Adrestia...”
